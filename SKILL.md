# Academic Essay Writing (Ruyin's style)

This skill captures Ruyin's writing process for argumentative academic essays. The focus is on **sentence-level planning** — how to decide what each sentence in a paragraph should do — because that is where the user has said her own process is still vague.

The skill does **not** prescribe an essay structure. The user decides the structure for each essay. If the structure is not yet provided, ask for it before drafting.

## Process overview

Writing follows three stages, in order:

1. **Choose topic** — usually a tension, ambiguity, or claim worth defending.
2. **Plan the structure** — the user decides this; do not impose a default.
3. **Fill in the structure** — for each paragraph, plan the topic sentence, then plan each follow-up sentence by its function.

The skill mainly addresses stage 3.

## Stage 1: Choosing the topic

A workable topic is framed as a **question or problem**, not a label. "Free will in Boethius" is a label; "How does Boethius reconcile divine foreknowledge with human freedom?" is a workable topic. Topics typically come from one of:

- A specific problem a text raises
- A tension or ambiguity an author does not fully resolve
- A claim worth defending, qualifying, or pushing back on
- An empirical or conceptual question with competing answers

## Stage 2: Planning the structure

**The user plans the structure.** Do not propose a fixed template. If the user has not yet decided on the structure, ask:

- What is the main claim or position?
- What are the major moves the essay needs to make to defend it (e.g., setup of terms, presentation of opposing view, evidence, response, etc.)?
- In what order should those moves go?
- Where should limitations or counterarguments sit? (The user typically places them near the end, before the conclusion, but this is her preference, not a rule.)

Once the user provides the paragraph plan, move to stage 3.

## Stage 3: Sentence-level planning

This is the core of the skill. After the topic sentence, every following sentence has a **function** to perform. Choose the function first, then write the sentence.

### Step 3a: The first sentence after the topic sentence

The topic sentence states a claim. The next sentence's job is to begin the **logic chain** — the first step of the mechanism that makes the claim work. Explain *how* the claim works in plain terms before bringing in evidence.

> Example: "Biological invasions by exotic species are an escalating threat to biodiversity. *[Next sentence:]* Invasive species can displace native ones and irrevocably alter ecosystems."

The first follow-up sentence does not yet introduce evidence. It explains the mechanism.

### Step 3b: Choosing the paragraph mode

Different paragraphs do different work. Identify which mode the paragraph is in, because the sentence sequence differs:

**Mode A — New claim with new evidence or reasoning:**
Topic sentence → Mechanism → Evidence or reasoning → Explanation → Implication for the larger argument.

**Mode B — Defining a concept the argument will rely on:**
Topic sentence stating the concept → Definition → Worked example or illustration → Why the definition matters for what comes next.

**Mode C — Linking back / synthesis:**
Topic sentence that names the connection → Re-state the earlier point in compressed form → Explain how the new material addresses it → State what is now resolved.

**Mode D — Conclusion paragraph:**
Do not introduce new evidence. Conclude across multiple **dimensions** of the argument: what was shown, what limitations remain, what stays open. Each dimension gets its own sentence or short cluster. Plan the dimensions first, then write to them.

**Mode E — Limitation / critical paragraph:**
Concede the strength of the argument first → Identify the unverified assumption or unaddressed gap → Name the consequence of that gap → Soften with hedged language ("may be limited," "remains an unresolved area").

### Step 3c: Integrating quotes or source material — only when material exists

**This step applies only when source material has been gathered before drafting.** If the user is writing without source material, skip this step entirely and rely on Mode A reasoning.

When source material is available — e.g., the user has read a primary text and marked sharp, central sentences while reading — those marked passages become candidate quotes. The selection happens during reading, not during drafting.

When writing a paragraph that uses a quote, **build the framing sentence around the quote, not the other way around**:

1. Identify what the quote *does* (defines a term, states the key move, draws a distinction, etc.).
2. Write a framing sentence that names that function and introduces the quote.
3. Drop in the quote with citation.
4. (Optional) Add a short interpretive sentence that pulls out what matters for the larger argument.

> Pattern: "[Author] [verb of argumentative action] by [what the quote does]: '[quote]' (citation). This [implication for the argument]."

If source material exists but no specific quote was pre-marked for this paragraph, paraphrase rather than forcing in a quote.

### Step 3d: Planning a sentence that connects back to earlier material

These sentences do structural work. To write one well: **re-read the earlier paragraph being linked to**, find the exact concept or term used there, and use that same term in the linking sentence. Working from memory often produces fuzzy links; re-reading produces sharp ones.

### Step 3e: When the mechanism is hard to explain

If a step in the logic chain is abstract or counterintuitive, **insert an example or piece of data immediately after the confusing claim** — not at the start of the paragraph and not at the end as decoration. The example's job is to rescue the reader at the moment they are most likely to lose the thread.

### Step 3f: When stuck on a sentence

In order, try:

1. Re-read the topic sentence of the current paragraph. Does the stuck sentence still serve it?
2. Re-read the framing of the whole essay. Does the paragraph still serve the larger argument?
3. Identify the missing logical link. The block is almost always a missing logical step, not a missing word.
4. If the mechanism is hard to articulate, look for a concrete example or piece of data to ground it.

## Voice and diction

Preserve a formal, hedged academic register:

- **Hedged claims for limitations**: "may be limited," "remains an unresolved area," "leaves open," "is not without limitations."
- **Strong verbs for argumentative moves**: challenges, distinguishes, asserts, contends, preempts, refines, reconciles.
- **Academic transitions**: Thus, However, Additionally, Moreover, Yet, By contrast, Conversely, As a result, In conclusion.
- **Cause/effect connectors are central**: according to, contributes to, follows that, gives rise to, as a result.
- **Quote integration verbs** (when applicable): writes, states, observes, argues, emphasizes, explains, clarifies.
- **Sentence rhythm**: mix longer analytical sentences with shorter punctuating ones. Don't let every sentence run to three clauses.

For argumentative claim-sentences, the eight TOEFL reason-patterns can be drawn on as scaffolding:

- "X could enable sb to do sth."
- "X could furnish sb with sth."
- "X could give sb an insight into sth."
- "X could play a major role in / be instrumental in sth."
- "X could correlate to/with sth closely."
- "X could reduce sb to sth."
- "X could be responsible for sth."
- "X is more likely to do sth."

Use these sparingly in formal academic prose — they are scaffolding, not a target style.

## Things to avoid

- **Do not let a quote stand alone.** Always frame it with a sentence naming what it does, and follow it with at least a brief interpretation if the quote is doing heavy lifting.
- **Do not introduce new evidence in the conclusion.** Conclusions conclude across dimensions of what has been argued.
- **Do not write linking sentences from memory** when you could re-read the earlier paragraph and use its exact terms.
- **Do not skip the mechanism sentence** right after the topic sentence in favor of jumping straight to evidence. The reader needs the "how" before the "proof."
- **Do not over-list.** Argumentative academic prose runs in paragraphs. Numbered lists are appropriate only for framing questions or enumerating items the essay will address, not for the body of an argument.
- **Do not impose a structure the user has not chosen.** Ask if it is unclear.

## Quick sentence-function checklist

When planning each sentence after the topic sentence, ask: **what is this sentence's job?**

- Mechanism (explain how)
- Definition (introduce a term)
- Evidence / quote (bring textual support — only if material exists)
- Quote framing (set up a quote)
- Quote interpretation (pull out what matters)
- Example (rescue an abstract claim)
- Link-back (connect to an earlier concept)
- Concession (acknowledge a strength before pushing back)
- Limitation (name an unverified assumption or unresolved tension)
- Implication (state what the claim means for the larger argument)
- Transition (move to the next paragraph's concern)

If a sentence isn't doing one of these jobs, it probably shouldn't be there.

AIGC Detection Reduction (for submitted drafts)
This section captures adjustments that effectively lower AI-detection scores (tested against Turnitin). Apply when a draft is complete and ready for submission.
What actually moves the needle
Detectors score on statistical predictability at the token level — specifically, how closely the text matches probability distributions from LLM output. The effective interventions are:

Sentence-length variance (highest impact) — mix short punchy sentences (6–10w) into longer analytical ones. Target a standard deviation of ~8–9 across all sentence lengths. A cluster of sentences all running 25–40 words is a strong flag.
Vocabulary simplification (moderate impact) — replace high-probability LLM words with simpler or slightly unexpected alternatives. Examples from practice: "characterised by → known for", "operationalised → put into practice", "juxtaposition → putting side by side", "consolidated → brought together", "demonstrate through dynamic modelling → show using modelling", "elevated → raised", "professionalises → raises the quality of".
Breaking parallel structures (moderate impact) — LLMs default to symmetric constructions ("X, whereas Y" / "on one hand... on the other"). Replace some with asymmetric phrasing or split into separate sentences.
Varying overused content words — run a frequency check after drafting. Words appearing 10+ times in ~3,000 words are flags. Rotate synonyms: "competitiveness → edge / strength", "framework → approach / model / system", "contrast → difference / split / gap", "logic → reason / idea / reasoning".
Connector mixing (low–moderate impact) — use simple connectors ("So", "Still", "On top of that", "Instead") for ordinary statements; reserve complex connectors ("Therefore", "Conversely", "Consequently") for major argumentative pivots only. LLM text overuses the complex set uniformly.

What does NOT help

Typos and missing apostrophes — detectors normalise for surface errors and are trained on typo-laden human text. Neutral signal at best; if clustered inconsistently across sections, can become its own flag.
Re-ordering sentences — does not change token-level perplexity.
Synonym-swapping without structural change — minor effect unless combined with sentence-length variance.

Sentence-break logic
Do not break sentences mechanically for length. Break at:

Logic pivots — where the argument changes direction or introduces a new mechanism. A full stop is stronger than a semicolon here.
Emphatic moments — a one-sentence paragraph or a very short sentence (3–8 words) after a long one signals emphasis and creates burstiness. Example: "The idea is simple." / "The two strengths sit on different planes."

Avoid breaks that leave a sentence incomplete or awkward on its own — the break itself can read as a burden if the two halves don't stand independently.
Intro signposting paragraphs
The standard "Section One does X, Section Two does Y..." roadmap paragraph is a strong AIGC flag. Its parallel five-sentence structure is extremely predictable. Replace with a logic-chain paragraph that:

States the analytical move the essay makes (why it compares the way it does)
Names the two levels or angles of comparison without listing them mechanically
Lets the SWOT or conclusion "fall out" naturally from the stated logic

This approach is also academically stronger — it shows the reader the reasoning, not just the scaffolding.
